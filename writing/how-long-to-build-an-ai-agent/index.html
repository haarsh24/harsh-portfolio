How long does it take to build an AI agent?

By Kumar Harsh, 1 November 2026, 2 min read

The short answer: a focused AI MVP takes 10–14 days, an autonomous agent or a retrieval system takes two to three weeks, and reliability work takes one to two weeks. These are the typical timelines I quote as a freelance AI engineer. What moves a project inside, or outside, those ranges is mostly scope, not model choice.

Typical timelines

  • AI MVP sprint: 10–14 days. A zero-to-one web app with a streaming AI interface, sign-in, a database and payment hooks. Details.
  • Autonomous AI agent or swarm: 2–3 weeks. Multi-step reasoning, tool calling, human review and token budgets. Details.
  • Enterprise RAG and memory: 2–3 weeks. Hybrid retrieval, re-ranking, session memory and answer verification. Details.
  • Reliability and chaos engineering: 1–2 weeks. Fallback cascades, retry budgets, circuit breakers and a chaos test suite. Details.
  • Standard production build: 3–4 weeks. A full enterprise-grade build with docs and hardening.

For ongoing work, a monthly retainer covers fractional AI systems architecture and continuous shipping.

What makes a build shorter

  • One clear workflow or user journey for the first release.
  • A short list of tools or integrations the agent has to use.
  • Data that is already accessible and reasonably clean.
  • Someone who can review work and make decisions quickly.

What makes it longer

  • Many integrations. Each tool an agent can call has its own authentication, edge cases and failure modes.
  • Messy or private data. It has to be cleaned, chunked and permissioned before retrieval works well.
  • Human-review requirements. They add approval steps and interfaces.
  • Accounts, payments or multiple customer workspaces on top of the AI feature.
  • High reliability targets. They call for fallbacks, retry budgets and testing against failure.

Where the time goes

A build runs in four moves: scope, architect, ship and harden. Scoping gives you an estimate and a direct reply within 12 hours. Architecture comes before code: agents, tools, retrieval and fallbacks are planned first. Shipping puts a production-ready app live. Hardening adds guardrails, token budgets, observability and model fallbacks. Skipping the last move is the usual way to get a demo quickly that is not ready for real users.
